## Continuity and distinctiveness

Humans are animals with evolutionary continuity to other primates. Yet complex institutions, symbolic systems, technologies, and cumulative cultural achievements require explanation. The question is not simply whether our brains are bigger or whether we win every cognitive task. Small differences in cooperation and learning can have large cumulative effects across groups and generations.

Ayala contrasts biological continuity with distinctively human products and activities. His excerpt discusses evidence from fossils, comparative anatomy, and molecular biology; bipedality appearing before the major increase in brain size; manipulative hands; changes in face, skin, body hair, development, vocal apparatus, and brain organization. Treat the detailed evolutionary dates and some broad species claims as statements in a 1998 reading, not a current scientific reference. The exam's explicit definition target is culture, and the central argument is dual biological/cultural inheritance.

## Two definitions of culture

**Ayala:** culture is the set of nonstrictly biological human activities and creations. Examples include language, social and political institutions, ethical and religious traditions, art, literature, scientific knowledge, technologies, and ways of doing things. “Nonstrictly biological” does not mean supernatural; biological organisms create and transmit culture, but genes alone do not specify its particular contents. For Q7, put this definition in one sentence and circle it as requested.

**Miller & Wood:** culture consists of learned and shared ways of living and thinking. Learned distinguishes it from direct genetic transmission. Shared distinguishes it from one person's idiosyncratic habit. Enculturation is acquiring culture through learning, including imitation, observation, stories, guidance, institutions, and informal social pressure.

## Six characteristics in Miller & Wood

- **Learned:** formal schooling is only one route; much acquisition is informal or unconscious.
- **Adaptive:** culture helps people live in changing environments, but some cultural products can be maladaptive or dangerous.
- **Related to nature:** biological needs such as eating and sleeping are met through culturally variable practices. The need alone does not specify the custom.
- **Symbolic:** things stand for other things; conventional meanings need to be learned.
- **Integrated:** practices are connected, so understanding one aspect may require the wider system. This is the idea of holism.
- **Changing and interacting:** contact, migration, trade, communication, and institutions transform cultures.

Imitation copies behavior. Local/stimulus enhancement makes an object or situation more likely to be encountered and learned about independently. Observing similar behavior alone may not distinguish these mechanisms. The older reading's statements about nonhuman teaching should be understood as its historical account rather than a license to claim that all current evidence is settled.

## Biological versus cultural inheritance

Biological inheritance transmits genetic information through reproduction. Cultural inheritance transmits information through learning and communication and can cross biological family lines. A student can learn a technique from a stranger or a book written centuries earlier.

Cultural adaptation can be fast and directed: people build coats instead of waiting for inherited fur, aircraft instead of wings, and life-support equipment instead of a new physiology. It can modify environments to suit existing organisms. Culture does not make biology irrelevant; biological learning capacities make this transmission possible. Ayala's concluding view requires both dimensions. His excerpt also distinguishes a capacity for ethical judgment from particular culturally acquired moral codes; the detailed later ethics section is explicitly omitted from the assigned excerpt.

## Cumulative culture and the ratchet effect

An innovation becomes a platform for another innovation when social learning preserves it. A ratchet prevents sliding all the way back: newcomers can start where predecessors left off. Technologies, institutions, and symbol systems exceed what one isolated person could invent. Humans pool cognitive resources across contemporaries and across generations.

At the individual level, the differences in learning or social cognition may be modest. At the population level, reliable transmission plus innovation can produce a large divergence. The lecture's transmission-threshold analogy concerns whether innovations spread and persist; it does not say culture is literally an infectious disease. Lost explorers versus people with local accumulated expertise illustrate why individual intelligence cannot substitute for all inherited cultural knowledge.

## Tomasello and Herrmann: physical versus social cognition

Comparisons of young children and apes do not show humans uniformly superior at every task. Physical-cognition tests concern space, quantities, and causal properties. Social-cognition tests concern interpreting intentions, communication, cooperation, and learning from others. The paper emphasizes a distinctive human profile in the social domain and the role of shared intentionality.

Shared intentionality combines skills and motivations for shared goals and coordinated perspectives. It is not merely reading another individual's behavior for personal advantage. Joint activity can involve a common goal while each participant understands a different role. In joint attention, “you and I attend to this together”; it is not just two eyes aimed at the same object.

## Pedagogy and cultural transmission, 38:28-47:37

Tomasello presents a proposed evolutionary sequence, not directly observed prehistoric events:

1. Individual innovation with conservative social learning: an established successful method tends to persist.
2. More flexible social learning: a learner can switch methods after seeing another way.
3. Demonstrative pedagogy: a knowledgeable participant intentionally shows another what to do, often with gestures.
4. Normative pedagogy/conformity and collaborative innovation: learners adopt how “we” do something, including steps whose utility is not immediately transparent.
5. Reason-giving: teaching why a method works enables correction and adaptation when circumstances change.

Faithful transmission preserves opaque skills; understanding the reasons makes that inheritance more flexible. Teaching can work only if learners have capacities to benefit from it. Dates and hominin labels in the talk are proposed framing, not the main mechanism to memorize.

## Dual inheritance, 50:12-end

Children inherit a biological capacity for learning and a socially constructed environment containing languages, tools, institutions, and practices. Development brings these together. A child with no cultural partners would not independently reinvent the accumulated culture. A language is learned using biological capacities for communication and conceptualization within a particular linguistic community. Tomasello explicitly distinguishes his account from endorsing a Chomskyan universal grammar; the course includes competing theories rather than one unanimously accepted model.
