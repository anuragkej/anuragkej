## §§1-2: replace a vague question with a testable game

Rather than settle “Can machines think?” by an opinion poll about word usage, Turing proposes an imitation game. His opening game has a man, a woman, and an interrogator separated from them. Communication is written/typed so appearance and voice do not reveal identity. He then asks what happens when a machine takes one participant's place. The familiar classroom form asks the interrogator to distinguish machine from human through conversation.

The test focuses on intellectual performance rather than whether an engineer can fabricate human skin or whether a machine can run faster than a person. Questions can range widely, including language, reasoning, humor, or ordinary knowledge. The concealed text channel controls irrelevant physical cues. Memorize the roles and information available to each participant, not just “a robot tricks someone.”

Turing recognizes that thinking could differ from human thinking. A machine that does not imitate humans might still be intelligent; the proposed positive criterion is not automatically a necessary condition for every possible intelligence. His original article and later standard versions differ in protocol details, so acknowledge the original imitation-game setup when asked about 1950.

## §§3-5: machines, digital organization, and universality

Turing limits the discussion to digital computers rather than every possible engineered organism. A digital computer can follow a rule book in place of a human computer. Its main components are a store for information, an executive unit performing operations, and control determining which instruction is applied next. Instruction tables can include conditional steps; stored information permits many different tasks.

Discrete-state machines move between distinguishable states. The description is an abstraction: physical signals are not literally free of continuous variation. Universality explains why one suitably general computer can simulate another given the appropriate instruction table and sufficient resources. The question is not whether the specific machine he had in 1950 already exhibited every ability, but what a suitably programmed digital computer could in principle do.

## §6: the nine contrary views

1. **The theological objection.** The objection ties thinking to an immortal soul given only to humans. Turing questions the restriction and argues that such a premise does not establish that God could not give a soul to a suitable machine. This is a response within the objection's framework, not a scientific proof of souls.
2. **The heads-in-the-sand objection.** The prospect of thinking machines is frightening, so one hopes it is false. Turing treats this as an emotional reaction rather than a reason that the claim cannot be true. Desirability and possibility are different questions.

3. **The mathematical objection.** Formal systems/machines have limits, including propositions they cannot settle. Turing accepts the limits but questions whether humans have been shown to escape all comparable limitations. Showing a particular machine fails on a specially chosen problem is not the same as showing no machine could model any human intelligence. Relate this to computability and the halting problem, without claiming Turing proved humans are computational.

4. **The argument from consciousness.** The objection demands genuine feeling or awareness, not merely outward production of a poem or response. Turing notes that demanding direct access to another being's experience risks solipsism: the same demand undermines knowledge of other human minds. Conversation can provide public evidence, even if the philosophical problem of consciousness remains. Do not infer that Turing proved experience irrelevant to every question.
5. **Arguments from various disabilities.** A machine supposedly can never be kind, make mistakes, be creative, appreciate something, or perform other human activities. Turing challenges unsupported extrapolation from the limited machines then available. Programmed rule-following is compatible with errors at the level of an inferred answer, and absence of a current demonstration is not proof of permanent impossibility.
6. **Lady Lovelace's objection.** The machine supposedly originates nothing and does only what we know how to order it to do. Turing emphasizes that consequences of a program can surprise its maker: knowing rules does not mean consciously anticipating all their consequences. Learning machines also shift the problem from explicitly supplying every final response to constructing a system that changes through experience.
7. **Continuity of the nervous system.** The nervous system is not literally a discrete-state machine, so perhaps a digital machine cannot imitate it. Turing distinguishes exact internal physical duplication from sufficiently similar input-output behavior. A digital approximation can be adequate to the discrimination task even if the underlying physical organization differs.
8. **Informality of behavior.** Human action seems too context-sensitive for an explicit rule prescribing the right response to every circumstance. Turing distinguishes rules consciously consulted by an agent from laws or mechanisms governing its behavior. Not having a simple verbal handbook for all human conduct does not prove behavior lacks an underlying mechanistic organization.
9. **Extrasensory perception.** Turing discusses telepathy and related claims as a possible complication for a fair test and imagines controlling the information channel. This is a historical part of the assigned article, not a claim in this guide that ESP has been established. The methodological point is to prevent information leakage from deciding a purported intellectual comparison.

## §7: learning machines

Turing proposes starting with a child-machine and educating it rather than hand-coding every feature of an adult mind. He separates an initial organization, the education it receives, and other experience. Search and selection can improve machine designs; reward and punishment can guide learning but carry limited information without richer communication. Randomness can support search rather than being the opposite of all intelligence.

Learning changes a system's effective behavior without necessarily violating the rules governing how the machine changes. A teacher may not be able to predict every internal modification. This connects to Lovelace: an engineered learner can produce consequences not anticipated in detail by the engineer.

The “onion” analogy asks whether successive mechanical explanations leave some irreducible inner mental core or eventually account for the whole phenomenon. The “critical mass” analogy concerns whether an idea sparks further activity rather than immediately dying out. Turing labels such analogies suggestive rather than conclusive proofs. His positive proposal ultimately invites experiment.

## What passing establishes, and what remains disputed

Passing establishes conversational performance meeting a specified protocol and supports Turing's proposed behavioral criterion. It does not independently identify the internal mechanism or settle every question about consciousness, meaning, or embodied competence. The Chinese Room is one challenge to treating such performance as sufficient for understanding.

Turing's five-minute/70-percent prediction is a prediction in the article, not a universal definition that every test must use. Performance depends on time limits, judges, instructions, comparison groups, and the success threshold.

For the current course's Q21: L10 p. 35 and the Study Guide explicitly discuss LLMs as passing recent Turing-test-style evaluations, with 2023 and 2025 papers provided as optional follow-up. Do not give the stale stock answer that no machine has ever passed any such evaluation. Attribute the answer to the course's cited results and explain protocol dependence. That result does not make the philosophical interpretation unanimous, and this guide is not making an independent claim about every system or every test as of the exam date.

CAPTCHAs connect to the practical problem of distinguishing machines and humans, often by exploiting task-specific differences such as visual recognition. They are not identical to the full conversational imitation game, and a particular CAPTCHA's success says little about all forms of intelligence.
