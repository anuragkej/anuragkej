## Q1. Cartesian minds

**Claim 1:** Each mind is distinct from any body.

**Claim 2:** Each mind is an individual immaterial mental substance that exists as a substance in its own right.

Do not replace either claim with the uninformative statement that bodies exist. [Chapter 2; L02.]

## Q2. Methodological naturalism

Methodological naturalism is the philosophical thesis that natural science requires no specific attitude toward religion and can be practiced by religious believers as well as atheists or agnostics. Scientific investigation uses natural explanations and scientific methods; that methodological commitment does not require a particular personal religious belief. [Chapter 3; L03 p. 14.]

## Q3. Ontological naturalism

Ontological naturalism is the philosophical thesis that reality consists of the natural world and contains no supernatural realm or entities. It concerns what exists, whereas methodological naturalism concerns how inquiry proceeds. Human culture and institutions can be part of nature in this sense. [Chapter 3.]

## Q4. Boden

According to Margaret Boden (2006), cognitive science is “the interdisciplinary study of mind, informed by theoretical concepts drawn from computer science and control theory.” It is catholic in covering all aspects of mind and behavior, drawing on many disciplines, and relying on more than one kind of theory. Give at least two of the three, but learn all three. [Chapter 4; Boden, printed pp. 9 and 12.]

## Q5. Scientism

Scientism is a philosophical thesis that extends scientific explanation/expression into the claim that everything worth expressing or knowing can be captured scientifically. In the course's contrast, ontological naturalism says that everything is natural without requiring science to replace all worthwhile artistic, personal, or ethical expression. For example, a naturalistic account of music need not replace what the music expresses. The terminology is contested, as the question itself notes. [Chapter 3; Flanagan; L03.]

## Q6. Double dissociation

A double dissociation occurs when one case is impaired on function A but relatively spared on B, while another case shows the reverse pattern. The crossing supports at least partial functional separability and makes a simple account based solely on one task being harder less plausible. It does not prove complete independence or a perfect one-to-one localization. Use Gage's executive problems and relative sparing of other functions against Zasetsky's posterior deficits and preserved goal-directed perseverance. [Chapter 3; discussion D02 material.]

## Q7. Ayala's culture

Culture is the set of nonstrictly biological human activities and creations. Circle that sentence if reproducing the requested answer format. Examples: languages, legal systems, scientific theories, art, ethical traditions, universities, and technologies. Explain examples only as needed; do not replace Ayala's definition with only Miller & Wood's learned/shared formulation. [Chapter 5; Ayala, PDF p. 5.]

## Q8. Joint attention observations

The child shares an activity by shifting attention between the object and adult, looking toward the adult's face for a response; during picture-book interaction she follows pointing, looks back to her mother, and points herself. The chimpanzee is interested in the ball and pictures but does not show the same partner-referencing and communicative sharing in the shown clips. The contrast is coordinated, triadic attention among self, partner, and object versus parallel interest in an object. Do not turn this into a general essay on all social cognition or make an unsupported claim about every ape. [Chapter 5; Tomasello about 10:00-14:30.]

## Q9. Finite Boolean function

A finite Boolean function is a mathematical function with finitely many input variables and finitely many output variables, each of which takes a value from a finite set; the Boolean restriction is that each such variable takes one of exactly two possible values, usually 0 and 1. Thus its input-output mapping can be fully enumerated. Separate the finiteness conditions from the binary condition. [Chapter 7; L07, pp. 7-9.]

## Q10. Combinatorial and sequential

A combinatorial circuit has no relevant internal state: after its specified delay, outputs are determined by current inputs. A sequential circuit maintains state, so its outputs can depend on both current inputs and the history encoded in that state. Under the standard compositional discipline, a combinatorial circuit cannot include a sequential component (TF1: no/false); a sequential circuit can include combinatorial components (TF2: yes/true), such as next-state and output logic. [Chapters 8-9.]

## Q11. FSM plus diagram

An FSM has a finite state set, input and output symbols, a specified initial state, a transition function N(x,q), and an output function F(x,q), with the Moore restriction F(q) as a special case. Each step produces the appropriate output and updates the state according to the current input and state. Draw the two-state toggle from Chapter 9: x = 0 self-loops on OFF and ON; x = 1 switches between them; outputs are 0 and 1 respectively. State explicitly that it has two states. ## Q12. Four synchronous conditions

A circuit is a synchronous sequential circuit if:

- Every component is either a register or a combinational circuit, and at least one component is a register.
- All registers receive the same clock signal.
- Every cyclic path contains at least one register.
- Register inputs meet the dynamic discipline: they remain stable throughout the required setup/hold aperture around the active clock edge.

This four-bullet form includes both the textbook's four structural conditions (the first bullet combines two) and L08's timing condition. If asked for the textbook's list verbatim, separate the two parts of the first bullet and present timing afterward as the additional operating requirement. [Chapter 9; Harris PDF pp. 28 and 33; L08 p. 28.]

## Q13. Register

A register is clocked storage that captures its data input as its new internal state on the rising edge of the clock and retains that state between rising edges, provided its input satisfies the setup and hold requirements. Its output represents the stored value, subject to the specified physical clock-to-output delay. Do not describe an entire HIGH level as the capture interval. [L08 p. 27.]

## Q14. TM versus FSM and the operator

A TM consists of a finite control, read/write head, and unbounded tape of cells over a finite alphabet. Its rule table maps current state and scanned symbol to a write action, head movement, and next state or halt. An FSM has finite internal storage; a TM combines finite control with unbounded potential external storage.

An FSM receives input symbols/values over successive steps and produces outputs as it runs; the operator supplies and observes those values at the interface. A TM receives an encoded input word prepared on its tape; the operator sets the initial configuration and starts it, then reads the output word when it halts. It may fail to halt on some inputs, so there need not be a completed answer. [Chapter 10; L09 pp. 10-13.]

## Q15. M implements F

For each natural-number input x in the stipulated domain, start M with the specified encoding of x. M implements the total function F if it halts and leaves an encoding of y = F(x) as its output for every such x. In the broader partial-function case, the domain consists of inputs on which the computation halts. [Chapter 10.]

## Q16. Functional equivalence

M1 and M2 are functionally equivalent if and only if, for every input word x, M1 halts on x exactly when M2 halts on x, and whenever they halt they produce the same output word. Agreement only on the intersection of their halting inputs is insufficient. [L09 p. 17.]

## Q17. UTM

A UTM is a TM U that takes (1) an encoded description of a TM M and (2) input data x for M, and simulates M on x. For each M, U with M's description fixed is functionally equivalent to M: same halting inputs and same answers on those inputs. UTMs form a class; universality does not identify one unique machine. [L09 pp. 20-22.]

## Q18. VNM and TM

Both idealized models implement rule-governed computation with stored information. A VNM has addressable memory, multiple registers, and richer primitive instructions; a basic TM uses a read/write head moving along tape and a very small set of elementary actions. For the idealized models, the class of VNM-computable functions is exactly equal to the class of Turing-computable functions. This equality does not imply equal speed or unlimited physical memory in an actual computer. [L09 p. 25; Haugeland.]

## Q19. The three important facts

1. Technologies exist such that, in principle, for any finite function F, a physical device can be constructed that implements F within bounded engineering parameters: propagation delay T, power consumption W, failure rate epsilon, mass M, production cost C, etc. [L07 p. 27.]
2. Technologies exist such that, in principle, for any FSM M = <F,N>, a physical device can be constructed that implements M within bounded engineering parameters: clock frequency f, power consumption W, failure rate epsilon, mass m, cost C, etc. Multiple realizations are possible. [L08 p. 31.]
3. Technologies exist for the industrial production of physical devices that implement universal programmable computers within bounded engineering parameters: clock frequency f, power consumption W, failure rate epsilon, mass m, cost C, etc. Multiple realizations of a given abstract computational system are routine. [L09 p. 29.]

Learn the progression and the bounded-parameter language, not merely “computers can do lots of things.”

## Q20. Church-Turing

Every effectively computable function is computable by a TM. Independently developed formal accounts of effective computation converge on the same function class, and known effective procedures can be represented within that class. Equivalence among formal models is mathematically provable; identifying them with the informal notion of all effective procedures is why the broader claim is a thesis. [Chapter 11; L10 pp. 5-8.]

## Q21. Turing Test

A separated interrogator communicates through text with hidden participants and tries to identify the machine, in a version of Turing's imitation game. Turing proposed this operational comparison in place of an ill-defined debate over whether machines think. Successful performance supports his behavioral criterion; it does not settle all disputes about consciousness or understanding. Mention the original man/woman game before the machine substitution when outlining the 1950 proposal. For the course's current answer, the slides describe recent LLM evaluations as passes, with interpretation depending on the protocol; do not say no system has ever passed any such test. [Chapter 12.]

## Q22. Chinese Room and AI

An operator who lacks Chinese understanding manipulates Chinese symbols using a formal rule book, producing responses that can satisfy a Chinese-speaking interrogator. Searle argues that syntactic rule execution is insufficient for semantics or understanding, challenging strong AI and the sufficiency of Turing-Test performance. Weak AI uses programs as modeling tools; strong AI claims the properly programmed system literally has mental states. The CRA attacks strong AI, not weak AI; it is compatible with the weak-AI project but does not establish the success of a particular system, and it does not support strong AI. Explain three objections: the Churchlands' challenge to the intuition, the systems reply distinguishing component from whole, and the robot reply adding grounded perception/action. [Chapter 13.]

## Q23. Both McCarthy definitions

Intelligence is the computational part of the ability to achieve goals in the world. Artificial intelligence is the science and engineering of making intelligent machines, especially intelligent computer programs. Label the two answers explicitly. [L10 p. 15.]
