**Core question:** How are mental states, events, and processes related to physical states, events, and processes? Feeling nervous and observing someone sweat concern the same situation from different perspectives, but the distinction between perspectives does not by itself settle what a mind is made of. [L02; Cartesian dualism video; Flanagan excerpt.]

## First person and third person

First-person access is the subject's experience: feeling nervous, seeing red, intending to speak, or having a thought. Third-person access concerns publicly observable evidence: sweating, speech, behavior, neural activity, and physiological measurements. A third-person investigator can infer nervousness; the subject experiences it. Do not equate the mental with only conscious verbal thought, or the third-person perspective with only visible behavior.

An anthropogenic approach begins with human mentality and asks what extends to other beings. A biogenic approach begins with living systems and asks how cognitive capacities arise and develop. These are starting points for inquiry, not the claim that all animals are humanlike or that all living things possess the same kind of mind.

## Manifest and scientific images

The manifest image is our ordinary, culturally developed way of understanding ourselves and the world: persons have purposes, make choices, and live meaningful lives. It is sophisticated and revisable, not simply an ignorant or primitive worldview. Scientific discoveries can enter common sense. The scientific image describes ourselves through systematic scientific explanation, including biological and physical mechanisms.

The two images may overlap or conflict. Explaining the acoustics of a song is different from expressing what it means to someone. That difference does not automatically prove two substances exist. Flanagan's issue is how scientific understanding and the human search for meaning fit together. A scientific account of a phenomenon need not replace every artistic, personal, or ethical way of discussing it. [Flanagan, all four PDF pages; L02.]

Flanagan also distinguishes an earlier “original image,” the practical schemes by which people get around in the world, from the culturally elaborated manifest image conveyed through narratives, art, and reflection. Greater elaboration does not automatically mean greater truth. Sellars' philosophical task is to understand how these accounts hang together. The solid everyday table and a physics description of its microscopic structure illustrate descriptions that may serve different purposes without simply canceling each other.

## The two Cartesian claims you must reproduce

> **Claim 1:** Each mind is distinct from any body.

> **Claim 2:** Each mind is an individual immaterial mental substance, existing as a substance in its own right.

The first claim rejects mind-body identity. The second is stronger than saying mental properties differ from physical properties: a Cartesian mind is a thing/substance, not merely a description, feature, or activity of a body. Descartes contrasts thinking substance, res cogitans, with extended substance, res extensa. A living human is understood as a union of mind and body. In this picture, the mind's independent existence makes separation from the body conceivable, including the possibility of survival after bodily death. This is a philosophical position, not an established experimental finding.

For Q1, focus on the nature of minds. Saying only that both minds and bodies exist fails to identify the two claims. Descartes' methodical doubt and the cogito connect to the apparent certainty of one's own thinking; they do not automatically give a complete scientific explanation of mind-body interaction.

## Four causal directions

| Direction | Example | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical → physical | Light stimulates the retina; neural signals propagate | Familiar natural causal processes |
| Physical → mental | Seeing a display produces a visual experience | Perception appears to connect body and mind |
| Mental → mental | A worry leads to an intention | Thoughts appear to influence other thoughts |
| Mental → physical | Intending to raise a hand produces movement | Central problem of mental causation |

**The interaction problem:** If an immaterial mind is not located in physical space, how does it causally affect a spatial body? Descartes' interactionist dualism accepts interaction; it does not originally deny it. Therefore, raising your hand is not a simple logical contradiction of the statement that mind and body are distinct. The challenge is explaining the interaction consistently with the proposed natures of the substances and with a physically complete causal account. Princess Elisabeth's correspondence presses this problem.

The video uses Ghost: separation of a ghost from its body illustrates substance independence; inability to stop a living attacker illustrates absent physical influence; learning to move an object dramatizes mental-to-physical causation. The film supplies illustrations, not evidence that ghosts exist. A ghost's unexplained ability to stand on the floor already shows how a story can selectively assume physical interaction.

## Physical causal closure and epiphenomenalism

**Physical causal closure:** every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause. More cautiously, every caused physical event has a sufficient physical cause; the formulation does not require every event to be deterministic. If physical causes already suffice for the hand's movement, what additional causal work does an immaterial intention perform? This motivates questions about redundancy, exclusion, and whether a nonphysical cause is needed.

Epiphenomenalism permits physical events to produce mental events while denying that those mental events causally affect physical events. Experience is a consequence without a causal return influence on the body. This differs from interactionist dualism and from identifying the mental with physical activity.

Do not define causal closure as “the mind does not exist.” Closure is a claim about physical causal explanation. Do not define physicalism as epiphenomenalism: physicalists can regard mental causes as physically realized causes.
